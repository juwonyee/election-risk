## 2. Numbers most usable for the probability table

1. **Seats a new governor fills in the first 2 years.** Turnover runs about 19–21% of seats per year. Basis: mean tenure 5.3 yrs gives 1/5.3 ≈ 19% (NARUC/NCSL 2019), and 41 of 197 seated commissioners started in 2023 ≈ 21% (Fortnightly 2024). That gives **≈ 40% of seats in 2 years (derived)**. Applied to commission sizes:
   - 3-seat commission (29 of 51): **≈ 1.2 seats**. A majority needs 2.
   - 5-seat commission (18 of 51): **≈ 2 seats**. A majority needs 3.

   Under strict staggering with no early exits it is lower: 1.0 seat for 3 seats on 6-yr terms, 2.0 for 5 seats on 5-yr terms. **Derived:** if each seat turns over independently with p = 0.4 in 2 years, P(new governor's own appointees form a majority) ≈ **35%** (3-seat) and **≈ 32%** (5-seat). This is a rough upper-bound model. Staggering makes it lower, and partisan-balance caps can make a same-party majority impossible.
2. **Selection method:** about 11 of 50 states elect commissioners (≈ 9 for the electric-rate body). For those states a governor's appointment lever is **zero**. 2 more states (SC, VA) leave appointment to the legislature.
3. **Does a change in who sits on the commission change outcomes?**
   - Elected vs appointed: residential electricity price ≈ **13% lower** in elected states (Besley & Coate, 1960–97, cross-section).
   - Partisanship: share of Democratic commissioners goes with **≈ 13–20 bp lower allowed ROE, not statistically significant** (Dunkle Werner & Jarvis 2026, n = 3,390). Treat "an appointee shifts ROE" as a small, noisy effect: tens of bp against a ~10% ROE.
   - A consumer advocate is a larger lever: **−45 bp ROE** (Fremeth et al. 2014).
4. **Senate confirmation rate for regulatory commission nominees**, pooled over 1999–2016 (**derived** from CRS): **same-party Senate 69.5% (349/502 nominations)** vs **opposite-party Senate 49.3% (111/225)**. The gap is about 20 pp. Most non-confirmations are "returned" at the end of the Congress, not rejected. Median days to confirm: about 50–130 under same-party control vs about 90 under opposite-party control. The difference is mostly in rate, not speed.
5. **Board partisanship → decisions.** The effect is real but sits at the margin. NLRB decisions are >90% unanimous, and partisanship shows up in the about 20% of contested and precedent-setting cases. FTC merger votes split on party lines in only 11 of 83 challenges (13%). Use this for policies that need a *doctrinal reversal* (e.g. an NLRB rule): high probability once a same-party majority is seated. For "enforcement volume" pledges, use a smaller probability.

## 3. Caveats

- **Causality.** Besley & Coate and Dunkle Werner & Jarvis are both correlational. Elected-commission states differ systematically: smaller, poorer, Southern and Plains. The two studies even disagree in sign. Besley & Coate find elected states more pro-consumer on prices. Dunkle Werner & Jarvis find elected commissions go with *higher* ROE in within-state specifications, though identified from few switches. Older literature (Boyes & McDowell 1989) says selection method often "makes no difference".
- **Era.** Besley & Coate's gap is driven by the 1970–85 fuel-price shock. In calm cost periods the regulator matters less. Their numbers are nominal 1960–97 cents.
- **Turnover numbers** mix reappointments and new appointees. A "new start" may be the same governor reappointing someone. Seat counts and party caps vary by state. A state-by-state term table was not found in free form. Advanced Energy United's PUC portal is the place to build one.
- **The CRS confirmation rates** count nominations, not individuals or positions. Renominations and "extra" nominations inflate the denominator. Only 3 opposite-party Congresses are in the sample (106th, 110th, 114th). Data stop at the 114th Congress (2016), before the 2019 2-hour post-cloture rule and the Sep 2025 en bloc precedent. Both of these likely raise same-party confirmation speed today. For a Nov 2026 scenario, the en bloc rule matters most when the Senate majority matches the president.
- **Federal removal power.** In 2025 the administration removed Democratic members at the FTC and NLRB, and the legality was litigated (Trump v. Slaughter / Wilcox). If removal protections are weakened, "majority change" at independent agencies no longer depends on term expiry. **Current legal status not verified here.** Check it before using the term-expiry logic for federal boards.
- **Insurance and gaming regulators.** The only solid quantitative finding is the pre-election delay in insurer interventions (−78%; Leverty & Grace). No peer-reviewed effect of elected vs appointed insurance commissioners on premiums was found, and no empirical study of gaming commissions was found.
- **Magnitudes not obtained** (paywall): Lim & Yurukoglu (conservative environments → higher ROE), Quast 2008, Semet 2016 panel probabilities, the McCarty & Razaghian hazard ratios, and the elected-commissioner coefficient in Fremeth et al.
